The honest version of "I use AI in my SEO workflow" isn't that AI writes my articles. It's that AI now does the parts of the process that used to eat the most time without needing much judgment — and I kept every part that does.

## Where AI actually helps

**Research and clustering.** Turning a broad topic into a list of related questions, subtopics, and search intents used to be an hour of manual digging. AI compresses that into a first-pass list I then edit down — removing anything that doesn't match what I actually know the audience asks.

**Outline structuring.** Once I know what a piece needs to cover, AI is useful for arranging that into a logical draft structure. It's a scaffolding tool, not an authoring tool.

**Optimization checks.** Meta description length, heading hierarchy, internal linking opportunities, readability flags — this is exactly the kind of repetitive, rules-based checking AI is reliable at, and exactly the kind of task I don't need to spend judgment on.

## Where it doesn't

**The actual point of view.** AI can describe a topic. It can't tell you what's actually true from experience, what a client's audience objects to, or which claim needs a caveat because you've seen it go wrong. That's the part of an article that makes it worth publishing instead of skipping straight to a search result.

**Community-sourced insight.** The best content ideas I get come from real questions in a community thread or a client's support inbox — language AI has no access to, because it hasn't happened yet.

**Final judgment on tone and claims.** Every AI-assisted draft gets a human pass before publishing — not for typos, but for whether every sentence is actually something I'd stand behind.

## The shape of the workflow

Roughly: real search-intent research → AI-assisted outline → human-written or heavily human-edited draft → AI pass for structural/SEO issues → final human edit for voice and accuracy → publish.

The failure mode I actively avoid is publishing anything AI produced with only a light edit. Readers can tell, and it's exactly the kind of generic content that ranks and still fails to build trust — the opposite of what this whole framework is trying to fix.
